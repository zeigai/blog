More data makes traders more confident, but not reliably more accurate. Past a few inputs that matter, extra charts, indicators and headlines mostly add noise, and the gap between how sure you feel and how right you are widens. 

Picture three monitors, a dozens indicators, two news feeds and a group chat. It feels like an edge. In practice it often means conflicting signals, slower decisions and bigger positions taken with false certainty. Here is what the research says, why it happens, and what to do instead. 

What the research says 

Across decades of studies, more information raises confidence faster than accuracy. 

Horse-racing handicappers. Psychologist Paul Slovic gave eight expert handicappers 5, 10, 20 and then 40 pieces of information per horse. On average they were as accurate with 5 as with 40, yet every one grew more confident. The extra data didn't make them better forecasters, it made them surer of themselves. 

Football forecasters. In 2008, Claire Tsai, Joshua Klayman and Reid Hastie found the same with people predicting American football results: confidence rose more than accuracy as information grew. 

Real trading accounts. Brad Barber and Terrance Odean studied 66,465 households at a discount broker from 1991 to 1996. Those who traded most earned 11.4% a year, against 17,9% for the market. Their picks weren't the main problem, the trading was, which the authors put down to overconfidence. 

Four ways too much data hurts 

Noise drowns out signal. Most daily moves and headlines mean little. The more feeds you watch, the more noise you react to, and the easier it is to miss the one move that matters. 

Signal contradict each other. Add enough indicators and some will always disagree. You end up picking the one that agrees with what you already wanted: confirmation bias with extra steps. 

Decisions slow down. With more to weigh, it takes longer to act. Waiting for one more confirmation means missing the planned entry, then chasing a worse one. This is often called analysis paralysis. 

Confidence outruns accuracy. Feeling well-informed leads traders to size up, widen stops or trade more often. More risk on the same accuracy is a poor trade-off. 

What actually helps 

The answer isn't less information. It's the right information, in context, with a plan. 

Pick a few inputs. In Slovic's study, five did as well as 40. Choose the handful that fit your strategy and ignore the rest. 

Write the plan first. Decide entry, stop, size and reason in advance. New data then confirms or cancels the plan instead of inventing a new one. 

Ask what a headline changes. If it doesn't shift the levels, trend or risk on what you trade, it's noise. 

Put risk first. Size from your stop and your account, not from how confident you feel. 

Treat sudden certainty as a warning. Feeling very sure after an hour of scrolling is a red flag, not a green light. 

Review your own traders. Your results show which inputs actually helped you. Drop the ones that didn't.
