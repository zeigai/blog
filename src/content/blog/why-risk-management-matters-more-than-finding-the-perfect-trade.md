Ask more traders what separates winners from losers, and they will talk about entries: the right indicator or the perfect setup. Yet long-term results depend less on how often a trader is right than on how much they lose when they are wrong. 

Economic announcements, geopolitical developments and sudden shifts in sentiment can move prices sharply, even when a setup looks promising. 

A trader cannot eliminate uncertainty or guarantee the outcome of a position. What they can do is define their risk before entering a trade, manage their exposure and prepare for possibility of being wrong. The aim is to ensure that no single trade puts the entire account at risk. 

Why the perfect trade is the wrong goal 

The search for the perfect entry assumes that enough analysis can make an outcome certain. It cannot. Even a strong setup is a probability, not a promise. When the European Securities and Markets Authority (ESMA) reviewed retail CDF trading across the EU, it found that 74-89% of retail accounts typically lost money, with average losses per ranging from €1,600 to €29,000. 

These figures highlight the risks associated with leveraged trading. Leverage can amplify losses, while excessive position sizes and poor risk controls can make it difficult to recover from adverse market movements. A promising idea is not enough without a clear view of how much capital is at risk. 

The maths of losing 

Losses and gains are not symmetrical. A 10% loss needs an 11% gain to recover. A 25% loss needs 33%. A 50% loss needs 100%. 

Consider a hypothetical strategy where each winning trade earns two units and each losing trade costs one. Across ten traders, four wins would generate eight units, while six losses would cost six. The result would be a net gain of two units before trading costs. 

This illustrates why win rate alone does not determine profitability. The relationship between average gains and average losses matters too. In practice, actual outcomes may differ from planned targets and stop-loss levels. 

Where risk is really decided 

Imagine two traders taking the same position on the same signal. One risks 1% of their account, with a stop-loss set before entry. The other feels confident and risks 10%. If the trade fails, the first has an ordinary losing day. The second has a drawdown that could take months to repair. 

One commonly discussed approach is to limit the planned risk on a single trade to a small percentage of account equity, such as 1-2%. The appropriate amount depends on the trader's strategy, financial circumstances, use of leverage and tolerance for risk. 

Importantly, a stop-loss does not guarantee that a positions will close at the intended price. During periods of high volatility or low liquidity, slippage can result in losses greater than planned. 

Major economic announcements, including inflation reports and central bank decisions, can trigger rapid price movements. Reducing position sizes, reassessing exposure or choosing not to trade around these events are among the ways traders may manage event-related risk.
